The Lighthouse Keeper's Journal

Chapter One

Every morning before the sun cleared the horizon, Mara climbed the hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room. The lighthouse had stood on this rock for ninety years, and in that time it had never once failed to turn its light toward the water. She meant to keep it that way for as long as the job was hers.

The sea that morning was calm, a rare gift after a week of storms. Mara logged the wind speed, the visibility, and the state of the lens, exactly as her predecessor had taught her. Routine, she had learned, was not the enemy of a good life. It was often the only thing standing between a sailor and the rocks.

By midday the supply boat would arrive, bringing letters, fresh bread, and news from the mainland that always felt a week too late by the time it reached her. She did not mind. Out here, time moved differently, and she had grown to prefer it that way.

Chapter Two

The letter that came that week was unlike the others. It was addressed not to Mara but to "the keeper," in handwriting she did not recognize. Inside was a single page, asking whether the lighthouse had records going back to the winter of a wreck that, as far as she knew, no one still living remembered.

She spent the afternoon in the small archive room below the lamp, working through ledgers bound in cracked leather. Dust rose in the low afternoon light. It took her three hours to find the entry: a ship's name, a date, and a short note in a keeper's hand from decades before her own.

That night, for the first time in months, Mara found herself thinking not about the routine of the light, but about everyone who had ever stood in this room before her, keeping the same watch, for the same reason.

Chapter Three

She wrote back the following week, transcribing the old entry as faithfully as she could and adding a note of her own about the lighthouse's current condition. It felt strange to write to a stranger about a shipwreck nobody alive remembered, and stranger still to realize that someday, another keeper might read her own words the same way.

The storms returned before the reply did. Mara climbed the hundred and twelve steps each morning regardless, checked the lens, logged the weather, and watched the light sweep out across a sea that did not care whether anyone was keeping records of it at all. That, she had decided, was precisely the point of keeping them anyway.

Chapter Four

Spring came late that year, but when it arrived it brought the supply boat's captain with news of his own: the mainland historical society wanted to visit, to see the old ledgers for themselves and perhaps take photographs for an exhibit on the region's shipping history. Mara agreed, though she found herself oddly protective of the dusty room she had spent so many afternoons in.

They came on a clear day in April, three researchers with gloves and careful hands, and Mara watched them turn the same pages she had turned months before. One of them, a young woman about her own age, asked whether Mara had ever thought about writing the lighthouse's own history herself, in her own words, rather than leaving it to the fragments other keepers had left behind.

Chapter Five

She thought about the question for weeks afterward, turning it over the way she turned over the weather each morning. It was one thing to log wind speed and visibility; it was another to decide what of her own life here was worth setting down for whoever came next.

In the end she started small: a single page, describing the view from the lamp room on a morning when the fog lifted all at once, revealing the water the way a curtain reveals a stage. She did not know if anyone would ever read it. She wrote it anyway, and placed it in the ledger, beside all the others who had once stood exactly where she stood.
